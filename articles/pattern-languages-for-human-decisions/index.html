Ways of Thinking

Pattern Languages for Human Decisions

Hermod Labs · 7 min read

  • Pattern languages
  • Reasoning modules
  • Ontology engineering

Experienced people often recognize a familiar problem inside a new situation. A proposal needs independent review. Two activities compete for a scarce resource. A favorable technical finding still needs authorization before anyone can act. The people and details change, while some of the relationships recur.

Pattern languages help us make that experience available for reuse. They connect recurring problems with approaches for addressing them, explain the circumstances that make those approaches useful, and show how one approach relates to others.

In the previous article, we described reasoning modules as manageable places to examine how conclusions follow. Pattern languages help us decide how to organize recurring pieces of that work. They give people shared ways to recognize a situation, discuss possible responses, and build a larger arrangement from related ideas.

We draw inspiration from Christopher Alexander and his collaborators, whose architectural work includes the 1977 book A Pattern Language. Their approach connects recurring design problems to adaptable configurations of relationships. A pattern language connects the patterns themselves, allowing people to combine them in designs suited to their circumstances.

For decision engineering, the useful idea is to preserve an approach together with the reasons for using it. In A Pattern Language for Pattern Writing, Gerard Meszaros and Jim Doble emphasize context, the problem, the forces involved, and the relationships to other patterns. Here, “forces” means the needs, constraints, and tensions that a response must address.

Consider an illustrative pattern for independent review in our supplier decision. Someone has prepared a technical assessment of a replacement material. Other people need to rely on that assessment before committing resources or placing an order. The preparer knows the work closely, but may also have overlooked an assumption or become attached to a preferred conclusion.

The recurring problem is how to obtain a credible examination of the assessment. Several considerations matter together. The reviewer needs enough expertise to understand the evidence, sufficient independence under the applicable requirements, access to the relevant material, and time to do the work. A reviewer who satisfies only one of those conditions may leave the problem unresolved.

The approach is to arrange a review by someone who meets the relevant qualifications and independence conditions, give them access to the evidence and assessment criteria, and record their finding with its scope and reasons. The particular people, standards, and documents will differ across decisions. The pattern preserves the relationships that make the review useful.

Its consequences belong in the description too. A review consumes time and may require scarce expertise. It can expose a need for additional evidence or lead to revisions. Applying the pattern therefore changes the work that must be planned. Those consequences help people judge whether the approach fits and what else they need to arrange.

This leads to other patterns. Independent review creates a need to establish reviewer availability. That connects to the broader problem of scheduling shared resources. The reviewer needs evidence whose source and scope can be examined, which connects to how evidence is recorded and maintained. A resulting finding may support an authorization, connecting the review to the rules governing who can permit an action.

The language lies in these connections. A pattern can help establish the conditions another pattern needs. It can introduce a new problem that another addresses. Two patterns may offer different responses to the same situation, with different costs or consequences. Following those relationships helps people work through a larger decision while keeping the reasons for each choice available.

In the supplier example, a proposed trial, an independent review, and a purchasing authorization contribute to the same overall change. Their relationship determines which evidence is needed, which activities must occur, and which conclusions can be used at each stage. A pattern language helps us describe that arrangement and examine how its parts support one another.

Pattern names also give people a useful vocabulary. Saying that a proposal needs independent review can bring a familiar set of concerns into the conversation. People can then concentrate on what is unusual about this case: perhaps the only available expert helped prepare the assessment, or the evidence concerns a different production process.

The name helps us find the relevant experience. The pattern description lets us inspect the reasoning behind it. Together, they reduce how much people have to reconstruct from memory while preserving a route back to the details.

Context remains central to applying the pattern. A review arrangement appropriate for one decision may be insufficient or unnecessarily burdensome for another. The consequences of error, the uncertainty in the evidence, and the applicable requirements all affect the choice. The people responsible for the decision need to judge which concerns matter and whether the proposed arrangement addresses them.

This is why we want patterns to explain their rationale. If a review exists to challenge assumptions, the reviewer needs access to those assumptions. If it exists to assess compliance with a specified standard, the standard and relevant evidence need to be available. Understanding the purpose helps people adapt the arrangement while retaining what makes it useful.

The connections between patterns need examination too. Adding an external reviewer may improve the available independence while introducing a delay. Moving the trial to accommodate that review may affect production commitments. A pattern language gives us places to describe those relationships; the particular decision model supplies the facts and requirements needed to assess the resulting combination.

Ontology engineering supports this work by defining the concepts the patterns use. A review pattern needs clear meanings for a reviewer, a finding, its evidence, and its scope. A scheduling pattern needs clear meanings for a resource, an activity, and the period during which the resource is required. Shared definitions help these patterns contribute to a coherent model.

Reasoning modules organize the claims and rules used to assess particular questions within that model. One pattern may influence several modules, and a module may draw on several patterns. For example, a module assessing whether a review requirement is satisfied may need to consider qualifications, independence, and the existence of an appropriate finding.

Phrase patterns play a related role in expression. An illustrative phrase such as “Reviewer [person] reviews report [report]” provides a consistent form for recording a relationship. The independent-review pattern explains the recurring problem the arrangement addresses and the conditions that make it useful. The ontology defines the terms, and the reasoning rules determine what follows from the recorded statements.

This connects pattern languages to our position on automation. Once we express the relevant concepts, relationships, and requirements in a form the software can use, we can automate checks across particular cases. A modeled review requirement can be checked against the recorded participants and findings. A changed reviewer assignment can lead us back to the qualifications and independence conditions that need reassessment.

The pattern helps people choose and understand the arrangement being modeled. The explicit model gives software a basis for checking its conditions and tracing its consequences. We want those contributions to reinforce each other, so that automation supports an approach people can explain and question.

Patterns also need to develop through use. In our work, a proposed pattern should carry examples of the situations it is meant to address and the reasons we expect it to help. Experience can reveal a missing condition, a recurring failure, or a useful variation. Recording those observations lets us refine the pattern’s scope and guidance.

For example, a review may have been formally independent but ineffective because the reviewer received only a summary. That experience should direct attention to access to supporting evidence. Repeated cases may help us describe when a summary is sufficient and when the underlying material must be examined.

As the language develops, we can preserve useful experience across decisions while keeping individual cases open to examination. People gain a starting point for organizing the work and a vocabulary for explaining why an arrangement might succeed or fail.

At Hermod Labs, pattern languages are part of helping humans think together. They make recurring approaches available for discussion, connect local problems to the larger decision, and preserve the reasoning needed to adapt what we have learned. A useful pattern helps someone recognize where to begin; its connections help them understand what follows.
