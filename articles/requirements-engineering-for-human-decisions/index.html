Ways of Thinking

Requirements Engineering for Human Decisions

Hermod Labs · 7 min read

  • Requirements
  • Dependencies
  • Evidence

At Hermod Labs, we work backward from the desired outcome. We begin with what people are trying to achieve and ask what would have to be true for that outcome to become possible. We then examine how those conditions could be established, what evidence would support them, and which people, resources, and decisions they depend on. Requirements engineering gives this process a structure.

In the previous article, we discussed how computational hermeneutics helps us interpret statements in context. Requirements engineering gives that interpretive work a direction. Understanding what someone means by “the material is acceptable” becomes useful when we can explain why acceptability matters to the decision and what would establish it.

Consider the supplier decision we have used throughout this series. A team proposes buying material from a cheaper supplier. The desired outcome might be lower production costs while continuing to meet quality and delivery commitments. Changing suppliers is one possible way to achieve that outcome. Negotiating different terms with the existing supplier or reducing material waste might also deserve consideration.

Working backward helps keep those possibilities visible. If we begin by treating a supplier change as inevitable, we can spend considerable effort organizing a transition whose value remains uncertain. Starting with the outcome gives us a reason to compare proposals and a basis for deciding what each proposal would need to accomplish.

The outcome itself needs interpretation and agreement. Suppose the team wants to reduce total material-related cost per conforming unit by at least five percent next quarter. People need to agree on the comparison baseline and which costs are included. Freight, qualification work, scrap, and inventory can change whether a lower purchase price produces the intended saving.

Requirements engineering involves discovering, clarifying, analyzing, agreeing, and maintaining the conditions a system must satisfy. NASA’s guidance on technical requirements definition describes translating stakeholder expectations into requirements and checking those requirements against the underlying objectives and constraints. The work includes establishing that requirements are understandable, feasible, and capable of being verified.

The relationship between goals and requirements is developed explicitly in goal-oriented requirements engineering. Axel van Lamsweerde describes how goals provide reasons for requirements and how refining goals helps people explore alternative ways of achieving them. That connection matters to us: a person examining a requirement should be able to understand the purpose it serves.

For the proposed supplier change, we can work backward from an acceptable production outcome. The material must perform adequately in the intended process. Sufficient quantities must be available when needed. The total costs must support the savings target. Purchasing commitments must have the required authorization. Each condition opens a smaller set of questions that people can investigate.

Take material performance. The requirement needs to identify the material, the process, the relevant product specification, and the operating conditions. We then ask what evidence would justify accepting the material for that use. A trial might provide part of that evidence, provided its design and evaluation criteria address the performance we actually need.

Working backward continues through the trial. It needs suitable material, equipment, an agreed procedure, and people who can conduct and assess it. If the purchasing decision depends on its results, the trial and review must finish early enough to inform that decision. Procurement lead times then connect the review date to the production schedule.

These relationships help distinguish a condition we need from an activity intended to establish it. Scheduling a trial establishes a plan. Completing it produces observations. Assessing those observations may support a conclusion about suitability. Each step has a different role in explaining whether the production requirement can be met.

Some conditions must hold together. An attractive price and suitable material still leave a delivery requirement to address. Other conditions permit alternatives. The required quantity might come from one supplier or an acceptable combination of suppliers. The model should preserve which conditions are jointly required and which represent different possible approaches.

This connects requirements engineering to hierarchical decomposition. NASA’s account of logical decomposition emphasizes deriving detailed requirements while examining relationships and interfaces. For our purposes, decomposition lets a person concentrate on one manageable question while retaining its connection to the outcome. Shared resources and dependencies still connect the separate branches.

We also need to examine the reasoning behind the decomposition. Satisfying every item in a list supports the larger outcome only to the extent that the list and its assumptions adequately capture what matters. A successful trial under one operating condition may leave performance under another condition unresolved.

Assumptions deserve an explicit place in that reasoning. A plan might rely on the supplier maintaining a stated lead time or on demand remaining within an expected range. Recording those assumptions exposes what the plan depends on and helps people decide where confirmation, monitoring, or contingency arrangements are needed.

Responsibility matters as well. Someone may be responsible for obtaining a capacity commitment, someone else for conducting the trial, and another person for authorizing the purchase. Assigning responsibility makes the required work actionable. The model must still distinguish an assigned obligation from evidence that the obligation has been fulfilled.

Requirements also help us distinguish commitments from preferences. Meeting the product specification may be mandatory, while lower inventory is desirable within an acceptable range. People need to decide which tradeoffs are permitted. A scoring method should preserve those decisions so that an attractive benefit does not silently compensate for a mandatory condition that remains unmet.

The way a requirement is expressed determines how people can assess it. “The material must be good enough” leaves the relevant judgment unclear. A useful statement identifies the required property, its scope, and the criteria for assessment. Where expert judgment is necessary, the basis for that judgment and the responsible role should be explicit.

We should also examine whether the chosen criteria serve the original outcome. A requirement concerned only with purchase price could be satisfied while total production costs increase. Returning to the outcome helps us detect a poorly chosen measure, an omitted cost, or a condition whose importance became apparent during investigation.

Working backward includes considering obstacles. The trial may fail, a reviewer may be unavailable, or the production line may already be committed. Discovering these possibilities can lead us to revise the timing, arrange another source of evidence, investigate another supplier, or reconsider whether the proposed saving is achievable.

The earlier ideas in this series support this work. Ontology engineering helps distinguish requirements, activities, evidence, assumptions, and authorizations. Reasoning modules organize the assessment of particular conditions. Pattern languages suggest approaches to recurring problems, while interpretation connects ordinary statements to the meanings on which those assessments depend.

Those connections need to remain inspectable. NASA’s guidance on requirements management emphasizes traceability in both directions and assessing the effects of changes. We want to move from an outcome to its supporting requirements and evidence, and from a particular requirement back to the reason it exists.

If the intended production volume changes, that traceability helps identify which capacity assessments, cost estimates, and delivery commitments need another look. If a trial result changes, it helps identify the conclusions that depended on it. A change can remain local in its source while having consequences elsewhere in the decision.

Explicit requirements make useful automation possible. Given defined relationships and rules, software can identify missing evidence, compare commitments with available capacity, and show which conclusions require review. Its results should preserve the difference between a condition that is satisfied, one that is contradicted, and one that remains unresolved.

The process moves in both directions. We work backward to identify what an outcome would require, then examine the available evidence and feasible arrangements to see what they support. What we learn may change the proposed solution, the requirements, or the outcome people are prepared to pursue.

Our aim is to help people understand what it would take to achieve something they value. Requirements engineering makes the connection between intention and action available for examination: why a condition matters, how it could be satisfied, what supports that judgment, and what remains to be done. The desired outcome gives the work its direction; the reasoning remains open to inspection and revision.
