Choose Checks Before Releasing Gift Kits

A print deadline, 120 gift kits, and a customer journey that has to work.

Tomorrow morning, 120 sample gift kits are due to leave for prospective retail buyers of a new insulated travel mug. Leah has allocated USD 6,000 to the campaign. The sample lets a buyer try the product; a printed QR insert invites them to place a preorder for their shop.

Before the kits are sealed, Leah asks the team to rehearse the buyer’s experience. A team member uses an actual printed insert, scans its code, checks that it opens the approved preorder page, and tries the intended checkout in the test environment. Leah wants to catch a broken step while the artwork, print, or checkout can still be corrected, before the problem reaches 120 recipients.

During that rehearsal, the journey fails.

The printer needs Leah’s correction instruction by 3:00 p.m. to keep the reserved production window. She must choose what to change by then; file changes, printing, and the final rehearsal follow afterward.

If the fault is discovered only after the kits are sealed, the fulfilment team’s planning estimate is USD 8 per kit to reopen, replace the insert, and reseal. Across 120 kits, that is USD 960 in potential handling work, before replacement printing or any delay. Sending a broken journey risks wasting the customer attention the campaign was meant to create.

Holding the launch is an option, but Leah wants to keep tomorrow’s dispatch.

Which checks can tell Leah what to fix before the printer’s cutoff?

One colleague proposes an immediate reprint; another wants more phone and account tests. Neither proposal yet tells Leah what to correct.

Leah uses Decision Lab to compare the investigations and check whether a choice can be ready in time. The marble cases give us a familiar pattern for that work.

An illustrative business story using the live gift-kit demo. The buyer audience, production timetable, and USD 8 handling rate are story assumptions. App inputs, calculations, and simulated results are identified in the walkthrough.

A reprint could leave the launch broken

An unreadable code is one explanation. A wrong destination is another. A checkout restriction is a third. Several can exist together: improving print quality could reveal that the newly readable code still points to the wrong page.

Leah therefore needs checks that separate the possible responses. Reading the printed code addresses readability and its link. Opening the approved checkout directly tests customer access without depending on the printed code. If the print is unreadable, the matching artwork can reveal whether its link is also wrong.

The familiar pattern is a decision about what to learn before committing. The next section brings over the original ontology from the marble article and fills the same conceptual structure with Leah’s business situation.

The contents and rules have changed. We previously considered possible locations of a heavy marble; Leah considers possible faults, including combinations. The familiar pattern helps us ask the right questions about a new business situation.

Buy a distinction that changes the response. A repeated failure report can leave the team unable to choose the right correction.

Tell Decision Lab what must be decided

Open Decision Lab, choose Workspaces, then New workspace, and open Choose checks before releasing gift kits. The example contains the possible faults, check predictions, and three investigation plans.

In the marble story, finding the containing bag required a different answer from identifying the exact marble. Decision / Goal & cutoff applies that same discipline. Leah needs a correction she can authorize. Another report that the journey failed would leave that decision open.

Under Guided questions, she sets the correction deadline to 16 elapsed minutes. From a planned 2:44 p.m. start, that represents the printer’s 3:00 p.m. cutoff. The reviewer is available from minute zero.

Decision / Case context fixes which situation the evidence concerns. In the marble cases, we needed the bag membership and heavy-marble rule. Here Leah identifies the printed insert revision, approved destination, and checkout configuration. A result about a different revision would need an applicability review.

Under Investigation / Explanations, she considers what could be true, just as we listed possible heavy-marble locations. Checks & predictions shows what each check could reveal about those possibilities. She reviews the supplied predictions and confirms the matching artwork and test access.

The original ontology’s situation, uncertainty, and required answer now have concrete business meanings in the case.

Decision Lab follows each plan’s possible reports and follow-up checks. It asks whether the remaining explanations call for the same correction, then checks access, resources, and time. Changing the questions can therefore change both what the team learns and the work it must do.

The colored-bag payout game added another test: would the result change which bag we chose? Leah asks the corresponding business question: would it change which correction she authorizes? Credits price the investigation work; the production window and possible late rework explain why a useful answer matters.

Recall the containing-bag case: once every remaining candidate was in the red bag, the bag question was settled even while marble identity remained open. Sufficiency applies the same standard here: the answer is enough only when no remaining explanation calls for a different fix.

Inspecting QUICK’s sufficiency cell shows why it fails. A failure report can leave several correction sets possible. In the earlier marble app, the quick comparison could fail because a report might be wrong; here, it can fail because the report is ambiguous about what needs fixing.

The earlier exact-marble search missed the handoff while gathering detail beyond the bag decision. AUDIT misses Leah’s cutoff while adding checks that supply no new correction distinction in this example. The common lesson is to judge the work against the required answer and commitment.

Leah chooses the targeted investigation. Its findings explain why it fits this decision under the supplied assumptions.

Check that the answer leaves time to act

In the marble app, a bag answer had to be interpreted and used before handoff. Leah opens Evidence timing to ask the same question about the printer’s cutoff. The chart includes preparation, checks, recording, interpretation, and the final choice, carried out sequentially by one reviewer.

Moving the marble handoff earlier could make an otherwise sufficient plan unusable. Bringing the printer’s cutoff forward does the same here: Leah updates the deadline and recomputes the reports. The information may stay the same while the available decision window closes. She then reviews another plan or holds the commitment.

See what the selected checks would justify

Before running the physical checks, Leah opens Evidence & sources / Simulated outcomes. Under TARGETED, she inspects square 110, the example with unreadable print and an incorrect link. Replay from start and Next report reveal how each result changes the supported response.

Reported results on the selected simulated path
Reported result on this pathWhat it means for Leah
The printed code is unreadablePrint needs attention; link and checkout faults remain possible
The approved checkout works directlyRule out a checkout restriction here; print alone or print plus a wrong link remain possible
The matching artwork has the wrong linkCorrect both print and link, then rehearse again

Read this sequence as we read successive balance reports. A weighing kept some heavy-marble locations possible and ruled out others. Here, the printed-code result leaves a hidden link fault possible; the later artwork result establishes that the link also needs correcting.

The useful distinction is about the response. A print-only correction would leave the wrong destination in place. The preview shows what each report would justify; Leah’s team then carries out the physical checks and reviews its actual results.

Leah keeps the correction window and releases the kits

Leah’s team follows the selected plan. Its physical checks return the same three findings shown in the preview: the insert is unreadable, the approved checkout works for the intended test account, and the matching artwork contains the wrong link.

By 2:58 p.m., the reports are interpreted. At 2:59 p.m., Leah approves the correction path: fix both the print and the encoded destination, retain the working checkout configuration, and rehearse the revised insert before packing. The printer keeps the reserved correction window.

The production team corrects the artwork and the print issue. The replacement becomes a new insert revision. The earlier checks concern the old insert, so the team rehearses the replacement. At 3:30 p.m., a physical sample can be read, opens the approved page, and completes the intended checkout. Leah reviews that result and approves pack-out.

The campaign still pays for checking and replacement printing. As in the payout-bag case, the information matters because it changes the choice: Leah authorizes both corrections while the production window is open. The team then confirms the revised journey before sealing the kits. The potential reopening job never becomes necessary in this case.

Decision Lab’s role is concrete: compare the investigation plans, explain which conditions support or block them, show when a choice can be ready, and make the consequences of possible reports inspectable. Leah’s team supplies the real checks and the final release judgment.

The next morning, all 120 kits leave on the planned dispatch with the corrected insert. Leah has kept the production window and sent the campaign out with a customer journey her team has rehearsed successfully.
