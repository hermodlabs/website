Your room’s symptoms can be repeatable.
So why are they so hard to catch?

In indoor cultivation, the room usually doesn’t go “bad” all at once. It fails as a repeating symptom: the same corner gets wet after lights-off, the same rack bay goes still after HVAC ramps, the same canopy layer condenses in the same window night after night. If the symptom is so repeatable, it feels like it should be easy to detect. But most teams only have global averages (a wall sensor, a controller mean, a single VPD/RH readout). Repeatable symptoms are often local, thin, vertical, and time-locked to triggers—so they can hide in plain sight while the dashboard stays green.

Common repeating symptoms (what shows up on the floor)

The fastest way out of debate mode is to start with the symptom. A symptom is practical: it has a location, a trigger window, and a recognizable fingerprint. Each one points to a first check you can run next cycle.

Symptom Signature Where it hides Why the dashboard misses it First thing to test
1) Lights-Off Fog Bank
  • A thin humidity/condensation layer shows up reliably after lights-off.
  • The same zones get wet without any obvious “spill” event.
  • Inside canopy depth.
  • Under benches.
  • Behind racks.
  • Near cold boundaries (walls, coils, supply ducts).
  • A wall sensor can sit in a well-mixed zone while a thin layer forms where plants actually live.
  • The room average stays calm because the failure is local and vertical.
  • Treat lights-off as a trigger.
  • Check a defined window (example: 0–45 minutes after lights-off) in the suspected zone, at canopy depth.
  • If it’s real, it repeats on comparable cycles.
2) Door-Corridor Plume
  • A door opening injects a narrow lane of different air (temperature/moisture).
  • It travels down a corridor, then curls and stalls when it hits geometry.
  • Problems track an aisle, not the whole room.
  • Along a single corridor or rack lane.
  • In wakes behind racks and curtains.
  • At the “first obstruction hit point” where the plume slams into a change in layout.
  • It’s intense but narrow.
  • The rest of the room can stay “normal” while one lane gets hammered.
  • Log or approximate door events.
  • Watch the first 10 minutes (0–10 minutes after a door burst).
  • Look for a corridor-shaped lane that repeats, then identify where it stalls.
3) HVAC Stratification Pocket
  • The room behaves like it has two stories: one layer warmer/drier, another cooler/wetter.
  • Pockets show up at the same phase of the HVAC cycle (start/stop/ramp).
  • Upper vs lower canopy layers.
  • Under jets or between jet paths.
  • Behind dense canopy where mixing collapses.
  • Sensors can sit in the “good layer.”
  • The failure is vertical structure, not a single number.
  • Label HVAC phases.
  • Compare the same canopy heights at the same phase across comparable cycles.
  • If the pocket is real, it appears at the same cycle edge.

If the symptom repeats, why is it so hard to catch?

Because “repeatable” does not mean “obvious.” Most symptoms repeat in ways that dashboards are not built to detect. They tend to be:

How symptoms repeat What that looks like Why your dashboard misses it
Local (small footprint) A single rack bay, corner, or aisle lane fails while everything else is fine. The sensor is elsewhere; the mean stays “in spec.”
Vertical (layered) Canopy-depth layers condense or stall while the top layer looks normal. Most sensors sit at wall height in well-mixed air.
Time-locked (trigger windows) It happens in a predictable window: 0–45 min post lights-off, 0–10 min post door, HVAC ramp edge. Dashboards smooth/average over time and ignore trigger alignment.
Geometry-dependent Behind racks, under benches, between jet paths—where mixing collapses. A single “representative” sensor can’t represent geometry traps.
Masked by “false green” control logic The room is controlled to a wall reading; the pocket can fail while control holds the mean. Control success ≠ pocket success.

This is why teams feel gaslit: the symptom repeats, but the measurement system isn’t looking in the right place or at the right time. The fix is not “more data.” The fix is better targeting: zone + trigger + window + comparable cycles.

How you catch a repeatable symptom (without guessing)

You catch it by turning “it feels weird” into a claim that can fail: same zone, same trigger, same window, comparable context.

Field What you lock down Examples
1) Where The exact zone — rack / bay / side / height.
  • “Rack B, Bay 3, lower canopy, wall-side.”
2) When A trigger and a window.
  • “Lights-off + 0–45 min.”
  • “Door burst + 0–10 min.”
  • “HVAC ramp edge.”
3) What The measurable symptom.
  • “Fog layer.”
  • “Dead pocket stillness.”
  • “Jet striping.”
  • “Two-layer stratification.”
4) Context Stage/setup so cycles are comparable.
  • “Week 5 flower.”
  • “Curtains deployed.”
  • “Dehu setpoint 50%.”
  • “Fans 60%.”

What the self-assessment checks (O/M/A/P readiness)

Even with a perfectly named symptom, pilots fail if the facility can’t support the evidence. The self-assessment checks four capabilities:

Axis Plain-language test Why it matters for pilot fit
O — Observability Can you measure in the pocket (canopy depth), with placement/cadence you trust? If you can’t see it, you can’t run a clean pilot. You’ll chase the mean.
M — Moisture control Can you clear moisture debt after triggers (lights-off, irrigation, load spikes)? If not, the symptom returns on schedule even if the room average behaves.
A — Air movement Can you exchange air through canopy depth (not just “fans exist”)? If not, geometry traps keep recreating the same pocket.
P — Proof loop Can you run one-knob changes on comparable cycles and produce a verdict? If not, you get “it feels better” and no defendable outcome.

Each axis can be present or missing. That creates 16 facility types. The self-assessment classifies your type and routes you to the pilot that matches it.

FAQ

What if I don’t know the trigger?

That’s common. The assessment can route you to an observability/baseline-first pilot that helps identify triggers and windows (instead of pretending you have them).

Do I need new sensors?

Not necessarily. Most teams just need one measurement placed where the symptom lives (canopy depth, in-zone) and aligned to a trigger window.

Is this a “score” of my facility?

No. It’s a readiness classification. It tells you what claims your current setup can support and what pilot won’t waste your time.
