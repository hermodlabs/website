1) Symptoms: what you actually see on the floor

In indoor agriculture, the underlying “disease” is usually some mix of layout + airflow + moisture + timing. But what you experience first is a specific, recurring pattern in the room. It's the symptoms that describe what shows up, where it shows up, and when it shows up.

We start with symptoms because that’s where real indoor grow problems live: on the floor, in a specific zone, at a specific time. It's not in a perfect room-average number. If you can’t point to what’s repeating, you can’t test anything, and every “fix” turns into guessing.

Symptom Signature Where it hides Why the dashboard misses it First thing to test
1) Lights-Off Fog Bank
  • A thin humidity/condensation layer shows up reliably after lights-off.
  • The same zones get wet without any obvious “spill” event.
  • Inside canopy depth.
  • Under benches.
  • Behind racks.
  • Near cold boundaries (walls, coils, supply ducts).
  • A wall sensor can sit in a well-mixed zone while a thin layer forms where plants actually live.
  • The room average stays calm because the failure is local and vertical.
  • Treat lights-off as a trigger.
  • Check a defined window (example: 0–45 minutes after lights-off) in the suspected zone, at canopy depth.
  • If it’s real, it repeats on comparable cycles.
2) Door-Corridor Plume
  • A door opening injects a narrow lane of different air (temperature/moisture).
  • It travels down a corridor, then curls and stalls when it hits geometry.
  • Problems track an aisle, not the whole room.
  • Along a single corridor or rack lane.
  • In wakes behind racks and curtains.
  • At the “first obstruction hit point” where the plume slams into a change in layout.
  • It’s intense but narrow.
  • The rest of the room can stay “normal” while one lane gets hammered.
  • Log or approximate door events.
  • Watch the first 10 minutes (0–10 minutes after a door burst).
  • Look for a corridor-shaped lane that repeats, then identify where it stalls.
3) HVAC Stratification Pocket
  • The room behaves like it has two stories: one layer warmer/drier, another cooler/wetter.
  • Pockets show up at the same phase of the HVAC cycle (start/stop/ramp).
  • Upper vs lower canopy layers.
  • Under jets or between jet paths.
  • Behind dense canopy where mixing collapses.
  • Sensors can sit in the “good layer.”
  • The failure is vertical structure, not a single number.
  • Label HVAC phases.
  • Compare the same canopy heights at the same phase across comparable cycles.
  • If the pocket is real, it appears at the same cycle edge.

2) Why averages lie

Teams argue because they’re each looking at a different “truth.” The person on the floor is standing in (or looking into) a pocket that’s wetter, stiller, or colder for long enough to matter. The dashboard is reading a sensor sitting in a better-mixed spot, then averaging that reading over time.

Because the bad zone is small compared to the whole room, the average can stay “fine.” The failure is local and geometry-dependent: canopy depth layers, corridor lanes, and shadow zones behind racks don’t have to move the room-wide mean.

What you notice What it feels/looks like Why the dashboard can still look “fine”
Wetter
  • Tacky surfaces
  • Localized condensation risk
  • A damp corner that repeats after a trigger
 Pockets are thin and local; the sensor may be in well-mixed air.
Drier
  • Leaf edges crisping
  • A “dry lane”
  • Localized over-drying under jets
 Averages wash out extremes when one zone is over-ventilated.
Windier
  • Leaf flutter
  • Directional blast
  • One aisle acting like a vent cannon
 Most dashboards measure temp/RH, not airflow where the pocket lives.
More still
  • Dead pocket
  • Musty smell
  • Slow recovery after lights-off or irrigation
 Mixing elsewhere keeps the mean stable while the pocket stalls.

The self-assessment exists because a pilot only works if we can measure the pocket (not the average) and compare comparable cycles.

3) Naming: how you turn a vibe into a test

The next step most teams skip is freezing the claim. Turning “it’s wet back there” into a checkable statement: same spot, same trigger, same window, comparable setup.

Field What you lock down Examples
1) Where The exact zone — rack / bay / side / height.
  • “Rack B, Bay 3, lower canopy, wall-side.”
2) When A trigger and a window.
  • “Lights-off + 0–45 min.”
  • “Door burst + 0–10 min.”
  • “HVAC ramp edge.”
3) What The measurable symptom.
  • “Fog layer.”
  • “Dead pocket stillness.”
  • “Jet striping.”
  • “Two-layer stratification.”
4) Context Stage/setup so cycles are comparable.
  • “Week 5 flower.”
  • “Curtains deployed.”
  • “Dehu setpoint 50%.”
  • “Fans 60%.”

Rooms don’t fail “at 2:13am.” They fail relative to triggers. The self-assessment checks whether your operation can log and align to those triggers.

4) DSM-MP: the field guide (and why pilots fail without it)

Even when everyone agrees something is wrong, the claim drifts: the location changes, the timing changes, the suspected cause changes, and after a few days nobody is talking about the exact same thing anymore. That’s why fixes turn into folklore.

DSM-MP forces three clarifications

  • Diagnosis: what repeats
  • Mechanism hypothesis: why it repeats
  • Testable fix: what would count as fixed (in the pocket, in the window, on comparable cycles)

Why this matters for pilot eligibility

  • If you can’t define the pocket, we can’t instrument it.
  • If you can’t align to triggers, we can’t compare cycles.
  • If multiple knobs move, we can’t attribute improvement.

5) Axes: why the problem exists (capability bottlenecks)

Two rooms can show the same symptom (say, a lights-off fog layer) but be in totally different situations. One room can measure it clearly and run clean trials; another can’t even see it reliably and ends up “fixing” the story instead of the system.

Axis Plain-language test Rule of thumb
O — Observability Can you see the truth where it happens (at canopy depth, in the pocket)? If O is weak, you can’t even declare “found.”
M — Moisture control Can you clear moisture debt after triggers (lights-off, irrigation, load spikes)? If M is weak, the room looks stable until a trigger pushes it over the edge.
A — Air movement Can you exchange air through canopy depth, or does it stall into repeatable dead zones? If A is weak, pockets re-form no matter what the average says.
P — Proof loop Can you run one-knob tests on comparable cycles and defend the verdict? If P is weak, you can’t declare “fixed.”

Each axis can be present or missing. That creates 16 facility types. The self-assessment classifies your type and routes you to the pilot that matches it.

6) Proof loop: the countermeasure

Microclimate problems stick around because teams get trapped in two loops: drift (the claim morphs over time) and false green (the dashboard stays green while the pocket fails out of sight).

Loop What it looks like What a proof loop forces instead
Drift “Wet spot after lights-off” becomes “humidity issues” becomes “airflow problems” becomes “probably fixed.” Freeze the claim: zone + trigger + window + context.
False green Wall sensor reads fine; the pocket persists somewhere else. Measure in the pocket, aligned to the trigger window.

A proof loop also allows the honest outcome: ABSTAIN. If the comparison isn’t fair, you don’t get to declare reality.

FAQ

Is this a “score” of my facility?

No. It’s a readiness classification. It tells you what claims your current setup can support and what pilot is feasible without guessing.

What if we don’t have perfect logs?

That’s normal. The assessment will flag missing comparability and may route you to a baseline/observability-first pilot. When the comparison isn’t fair, the correct output is ABSTAIN.

What do you need from me to take the assessment?

The symptom you suspect, where it shows up, what trigger seems to precede it (lights-off, door traffic, HVAC edges, irrigation), and a rough sense of how you currently measure (and whether you can place at canopy depth).
