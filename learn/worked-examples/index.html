Learn / Worked examples

See the relationships
behind a decision.

Everyday decisions become easier to examine when their objectives, evidence, constraints, alternatives, and tradeoffs are visible. These two illustrative situations ask what would count as an improvement and whose outcomes matter.

Inventory planning ↓ Insurance decision ↓

These are explanatory examples, separate from the applications available in our Tools section.

01 / Inventory planning

A cigar lounge prepares
for an event.

A lounge is choosing inventory for a members’ event. Guests want a suitable selection; staff need an order they can store and prepare; the organizer has a spending limit. “Better” needs criteria that account for each of them.

The criteria

Agree on what the order should achieve

Compare options against the desired selection, available storage, delivery timing, cost, and staff effort. A wider selection may add preparation work; a smaller order may leave fewer choices for guests.

Make the tradeoffs visible to the people affected.

The support

Make assumptions checkable

The event organizer checks the guest estimate. Staff check usable stock and storage space. The supplier confirms the delivery date. Keep confirmed information separate from assumptions when comparing orders.

Give each open question an owner.

The change

Trace a delayed delivery

If a delivery is delayed, compare using available stock, changing the selection, or checking another supplier. Revisit both the evidence and the criteria: an option that arrives on time may also change cost or staff effort.

Reconsider the affected relationships.

The question to carry forward: Which option best serves the agreed objectives, and can the people affected challenge the tradeoffs behind that judgment?

02 / Decision review

An insurance choice
needs a second look.

A small organization is reviewing insurance options before changing how it operates. It needs to make the intended outcomes, budget constraints, and responsibilities explicit before judging whether one option is an improvement.

The criteria

Describe the decision first

Describe the activities and situations to discuss with the provider, who is affected, and the constraints on the choice. Set out what the organization wants to compare so participants can question its priorities.

State whose outcomes the criteria represent.

The support

Keep questions beside the evidence

Record the documents and provider responses behind each understanding. Keep unresolved questions visible and assign responsibility for clarifying them before relying on the comparison.

An unanswered question stays visible.

The change

Revisit a changed activity

When the proposed operation changes, return to the descriptions and assumptions used in the review. Identify the affected questions for the provider before carrying an earlier conclusion into the new situation.

A changed situation calls for renewed review.

The question to carry forward: Do the criteria still represent the organization’s needs, and what evidence would be needed to compare the options for the revised operation?

A shared approach

Different situations.
Connected questions.

In both examples, the work is to state what matters, compare alternatives against evidence and constraints, and keep the criteria open to challenge. Decision engineering gives that reasoning a structure people can revisit.
