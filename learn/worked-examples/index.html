Learn / Worked examples

See the relationships
behind a decision.

A plan becomes easier to examine when its requirements, assumptions, and alternatives are visible. These two illustrative situations show how to ask more precise questions about a decision.

Inventory planning ↓ Insurance decision ↓

These are explanatory examples, separate from the applications available in our Tools section.

01 / Inventory planning

A cigar lounge prepares
for an event.

A lounge is planning inventory for a members’ event. The initial plan depends on an expected guest count, stock already on hand, room to store an order, and a supplier’s arrival date.

The plan

Connect the order to its purpose

The goal is to have a suitable selection ready for the event. A proposed order contributes to that goal only if it arrives in time, fits the available storage, and complements usable stock.

A purchase is one part of the plan.

The support

Make assumptions checkable

The event organizer confirms the guest estimate. A staff member checks the stock count and storage space. The supplier confirms the delivery date. Until then, those inputs remain assumptions.

Give each open question an owner.

The change

Trace a delayed delivery

If the supplier changes the arrival date, revisit the selection that depended on that order. Compare using available stock, changing the selection, or checking another supplier against the same event needs.

Reconsider the affected relationships.

The question to carry forward: Which parts of the event plan still have support, and which need a new arrangement before anyone places the order?

02 / Decision review

An insurance choice
needs a second look.

A small organization is reviewing insurance options before changing how it operates. The group needs a clear account of what each option is intended to address, what its understanding rests on, and which questions remain unresolved.

The plan

Describe the decision first

Write down the activities and situations the organization wants to discuss with its insurance provider. Use that description consistently when reviewing the options, rather than treating an option’s name as an explanation.

Make the intended outcome explicit.

The support

Keep questions beside the evidence

Record the documents and provider responses behind each understanding. Mark anything that still needs clarification, and identify the person responsible for obtaining and reviewing that response.

An unanswered question stays visible.

The change

Revisit a changed activity

When the proposed operation changes, return to the descriptions and assumptions used in the review. Identify the affected questions for the provider before carrying an earlier conclusion into the new situation.

A changed situation calls for renewed review.

The question to carry forward: What would need to be confirmed before the group could rely on the same reasoning for the revised operation?

A shared approach

Different situations.
Connected questions.

In both examples, the work is to explain what the plan depends on, make open questions visible, and know what deserves another look when something changes.
